The problem

NASA has decades of fire experiments in space. They are hard to find, compare, or trust at a glance.

Results sit in scanned conference papers and dataset tables, each with its own units and conditions. A mission planner who asks "how fast does fire spread in our cabin air?" has to read dozens of PDFs.

And the question is changing. Moon habitats are planned at lower pressure with more oxygen than the ISS.

The product

Flamescope puts every run on one map, with its source one click away.

The live site: runs plotted by oxygen and pressure, colored from blue to amber as oxygen rises (as the flames themselves did), with Moon and Mars targets ringed. Hollow means awaiting a second-person check.

NASA data

Built entirely on open NASA data.

  1. FetchNASA Technical Reports Server papers and Physical Sciences Informatics datasets (BASS, BASS-II, Saffire I to III)
  2. ExtractOne row per experiment run, in kPa, mm/s and m/s, keeping the value as reported
  3. ValidateMissing fields exclude a run, never rank it low; a second person checks every number
  4. RetrieveSource passages and runs searched in the browser, with no server needed
  5. InterpretAn LLM answers only from retrieved records; unmatched numbers are rejected

What the evidence says

What the evidence says, and where it stops.

More oxygen and higher pressure make thin fuels burn faster.

Shuttle runs over thin cellulose rose from 0.92 mm/s at 35% O₂ and 101.325 kPa to 5.47 mm/s at 50% O₂ and 202.650 kPa.

FS-0001FS-0005

Thick plastic never settled into a steady spread rate.

In all three thick-fuel shuttle flights the flame slowed with time, so short tests can mislead for thick materials.

FS-0006C-0007

Nothing here was burned at lunar habitat pressure.

The Moon target is 34% O₂ at 56.537 kPa; no run in the table was flown below 101.325 kPa. That is the top data need.

What's next

A fire-safety evidence base crews can question before they choose the air they breathe.

Next: every run checked by a second person, live answers from a server that holds the key, more NASA and partner-agency sources, and partial-gravity experiments as they fly.

The same approach works for any body of space-science results: one row per experiment, every claim cited, and honest gaps.
